How AI Transformed My Role as a Manager

Published on May 17, 2026 by Asim Khaliq

AI hasn't just changed the tools my teams use which keep getting upgraded with new capabilities — it has changed what I actually do as a manager day to day.

From Knowing Things to Enabling Outcomes

Early in my career, I added value by knowing things: the right configuration, the right framework, the right approach. I've watched that role become obsolete in real time. On a recent modernization engagement for New Jersey state applications, I ran into .NET configuration environments outside my core expertise. Rather than becoming the bottleneck while I climbed the learning curve, I leaned on AI tooling to close that gap quickly — and redirected my energy toward what was actually needed: unblocking the team, aligning product owners, and shipping on time with quality. That experience clarified something: my job is no longer to be the smartest person in the room about any given technology. It's to make sure the room produces great outcomes.

Shifting Priorities

That shift has changed what I prioritize. I spend less time on implementation guidance and more time on system thinking, architecture tradeoffs, long-term maintainability, the decisions that AI can't make well because they require organizational context and judgment. I'm also more deliberate about engineer growth in this new environment. I teach a web development course in the evenings, and what I see there mirrors what I see on teams: AI can help with the code and make a junior engineer sound senior. Part of my job now is helping people develop real judgment, not just fluent-sounding output. That new opportunity is what excites me now in terms of how to build confidence in teams to give their best.

New Metrics for Success

What I've stopped doing is using output metrics such as how many PRs were merged, how many tickets are closed — as meaningful signals for evaluation. In an AI-augmented world, those numbers are nearly meaningless. What matters now is the quality of judgment behind the work, not the volume of output. I've also stopped writing detailed how-to documentation myself; AI handles first drafts, and I review them. That time goes back to the people and strategic conversations that compound over time.

Patterns Across Industries

I worked across healthcare and public sector digital transformations, and in both cases the pattern was the same: the teams that adapted their ways of working — not just their tooling were the ones that delivered.

A Philosophy Aligned with Purpose

I've read Kevin's piece, "Our goal was never to write code," and it articulates this shift precisely. That framing — that engineering is fundamentally about solving problems and delivering value, with code as the medium — is exactly how I've come to think about it. I'd genuinely welcome the conversation with him about how that philosophy is shaping this team's direction, because that alignment is a big part of why this role stood out to me.
